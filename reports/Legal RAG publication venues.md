# Submit to AI and Law, armed with human validation

Springer's *Artificial Intelligence and Law* is the best target for the Legal Workflow Generator paper. Its scope fits, it has a JIF of about 3.1, a no-fee subscription route suits student authors, and its desk triage is fast (median 6 days to first decision) ([Springer AI & Law](https://link.springer.com/journal/10506)). IEEE Access and Springer *Discover Artificial Intelligence* are faster open-access fallbacks, at roughly $1,300 to $2,800 in fees. The paper's genuine novelty is not its components. Claim-level citation checking, corrective retrieval and as-of-date filtering all have 2023-2026 prior art. What is new is the task and the resource: business-facing, multi-statute Indian compliance workflows, each step citing a provision, covering the DPDP Act 2023 and Rules 2025. No published benchmark or system covers that ground. The headline result is that LLM-only generation produces 59 citations to non-existent provisions and 16.8% unsupported claims, while the pipeline brings both to about zero. That number becomes publishable only once humans validate it, because today an LLM is grading an LLM, partly from the same model family. Three days is enough time to *submit*, not to get accepted. Use those days for a roughly 200-claim human audit with agreement statistics, a cross-family judge, a clean held-out split, bootstrap confidence intervals and one recognised baseline, rather than for further system tuning.

## Prior work covers case law, US law and single statutes, not Indian compliance procedures

The related work falls into six clusters, and the paper must position itself against each.

**Legal hallucination.** The anchor citations are the Stanford RegLab studies. Dahl et al. found that general LLMs hallucinated on **58–88%** of verifiable US case-law questions ([arXiv 2401.01301](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.01301)). Magesh et al. showed in a preregistered study that commercial legal RAG tools (Lexis+ AI, Ask Practical Law AI) still hallucinated **more than 17%** of the time, and Westlaw's research tool **more than 34%**, despite "hallucination-free" marketing ([RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:empleg:v:22:y:2025:i:2:p:216-242); [Stanford Law](https://law.stanford.edu/publications/hallucination-free/)). Their definition, which counts as a hallucination any false assertion that a source supports a proposition, is the one this paper's verifier implements. In 2026 the field moved to claim-level checking. PARCEL (ICAIL 2026) labels 3,396 claims as Supported, Refuted or Not Found, and finds that models often accept unsupported claims and that plausible fabricated citations are the hardest case ([arXiv 2610.10971](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10971)). "Who Checks the Citations?" found that even an agentic GPT-5 detector reaches only 55.0% F1 on injected citation errors ([arXiv 2606.21155](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21155)). The only Indian work in this cluster is HalluDetect (EMNLP 2025 Industry), which studies consumer-grievance chatbots rather than statutes ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-industry.128/)).

**Statutory and regulatory RAG.** Existing benchmarks cover Belgian statutes with BSARD ([arXiv 2108.11792](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.11792)), US tax with SARA ([arXiv 2005.05257](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05257)), the Japanese Civil Code with COLIEE ([COLIEE](https://coliee.org/)), contracts with LegalBench-RAG (6,858 expert-annotated pairs) ([arXiv 2408.10343](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10343)), and ADGM financial regulation with ObliQA/RIRAG (27,869 questions, scored by Recall@10 plus the RePASs faithfulness metric) ([arXiv 2409.05677](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.05677v2)). Gokhan and Briscoe's NLLP 2025 follow-up shows that retrieval degrades when an answer needs several passages, and responds with learning-to-rank and citation-grounded answers ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.nllp-1.10)). That is the closest framing precedent for this paper's multi-domain split. GraphCompliance (GDPR) ([arXiv 2510.26309](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.26309v1)) and PrivaCI-Bench, which reports that RAG can *hurt* on GDPR questions ([arXiv 2502.17041](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.17041)), are useful data-protection comparators.

**Indian legal NLP.** This work is judgment-centric: ILDC for judgment prediction ([arXiv 2105.13562](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.13562)), IL-TUR (ACL 2024) ([arXiv 2407.05399](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.05399)), IL-PCSR (EMNLP 2025) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.00268v1)), NyayaAnumana (COLING 2025) ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.738)) and NyayaRAG ([arXiv 2508.00709](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.00709)). The nearest statute-level resources are ILSIC, which maps layperson queries to 500+ statutes as a classification task ([arXiv 2602.00881](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00881)), and IndicIPR-QA, which offers extractive QA over seven IP Acts ([arXiv 2610.40518](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40518)). The **closest direct competitor** is a February 2026 BITS Pilani preprint on domain-partitioned hybrid RAG for Indian law. It evaluated on 40 synthetic questions with an unnamed LLM judge, reported no inter-rater agreement, and left temporal validity and abstention as future work ([arXiv 2602.23371](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.23371v1)). The only DPDP paper found is an agentic data-anonymisation framework, not legal QA ([arXiv 2601.01101](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.01101)). A non-peer-reviewed industry benchmark, LE-BTL, covers income tax, GST and FEMA questions, so Indian tax QA is becoming contested territory ([Taxmann](https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/taxmann-ai-x-iit-kharagpur-llm-evaluation-le-btl-benchmark-study)).

**Faithfulness tooling.** The standard methods are ALCE for NLI-based citation precision and recall ([arXiv 2305.14627](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14627)), FActScore for atomic-claim decomposition ([arXiv 2305.14251](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14251)), RAGAS ([arXiv 2309.15217](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15217)), ARES, which calibrates LLM judges with a small human-labelled set ([arXiv 2311.09476](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09476)), Self-RAG ([arXiv 2310.11511](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11511)), CRAG, the closest analogue of the project's grader-driven context selection ([arXiv 2401.15884](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15884)), and the TACL survey on abstention ([arXiv 2407.18418](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18418)).

**Temporal validity.** This is now a busy subfield. Prior, Schultz and Grabmair (ICAIL 2026, best-paper nominee) treat "temporal validity as a hard constraint" over 312 German statutory QA pairs ([arXiv 2605.23497](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23497)). LawInForce-eCFR (AACL 2026) contributes 3,220 amendment-aware QA instances ([AACL 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.aacl-long.42.pdf)). A French tax study reached 98.3% accuracy with a date-conditioned retriever where static RAG failed ([arXiv 2608.09393](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.09393)). SAT-Graph RAG models versioned norms ([arXiv 2505.00039](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.00039v5)). All of these handle *amendment versioning*. None was found that handles *phased commencement*, meaning provisions that are notified but not yet in force, which is the DPDP Rules 2025 situation. That narrow angle is worth a sentence, though not a headline claim.

**Multi-agent compliance.** Existing systems check whether an artefact complies and return a verdict. Examples include software requirements in ACM TOSEM ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3785472)), Japanese APPI data transfers ([arXiv 2511.10925](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.10925)) and SMT-backed financial law ([arXiv 2601.06181](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.06181)). No peer-reviewed work was found that *generates* an ordered compliance procedure with a statutory citation at every step.

## The defensible novelty is the task and the dataset, not the components

Ranked by how well each claim survives a well-read reviewer, **the strongest is the task formulation plus resource**. The paper formulates multi-domain Indian business compliance as workflow generation, with a provision cited at each step, and supplies gold provision lists and a 4,888-provision corpus annotated with as-of dates, including the DPDP Act and Rules. That claim holds only if the queries, gold lists and corpus are released. At 100 author-written queries the set is small next to LegalBench-RAG's 6,858 or LawInForce's 3,220, so call it a "diagnostic test set" or "pilot benchmark" ([arXiv 2408.10343](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10343)).

**Second is the fabricated-citation finding.** The LLM-only baseline produced 59 non-existent provision citations and 16.8% unsupported claims. Checking whether a cited provision exists is a deterministic corpus lookup, so that number is crisp. It reproduces the Stanford RegLab concern in a new jurisdiction and output type, at a rate close to Magesh et al.'s more-than-17% for commercial tools ([Stanford Law](https://law.stanford.edu/publications/hallucination-free/)). **Third is the evidence that the components work.** The ablation path from 0.603 to 0.776 recall, especially the +0.125 jump from context selection, shows the verification and selection steps earn their place. Grader-driven selection and claim-level verification are still recognisably CRAG- and FActScore-style designs, so present them as engineering rather than invention. **Scope abstention** (10/10 out-of-scope refused, 2 false refusals) and **as-of-date handling** are real safety properties, but the evidence for the first is too thin and the second is too crowded a field to headline. The cost angle, near-zero unsupported claims from a free-tier flash-lite generator, lands only alongside a stronger-model comparison.

Against the closest Indian competitor the paper already wins on query count (100 vs 40), gold provision labels, abstention, temporal handling and the compliance focus. After the fixes below it also wins on judge validation ([arXiv 2602.23371](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.23371v1)). Cite that paper explicitly and contrast with it.

**Suggested title:** *Grounded or Fabricated? Step-Level Citation Verification for Multi-Statute Indian Compliance Workflows*. A benchmark-forward alternative is *[Name]: A Benchmark and Citation-Verified Agentic RAG Pipeline for Indian Regulatory Compliance Workflows*.

**Suggested contribution statement:**

> To our knowledge, we present the first study of citation faithfulness in generated compliance procedures under Indian regulatory law. We (1) formulate multi-statute compliance workflow generation, in which every step must cite a provision that supports it, and release a 100-query pilot benchmark with gold provision lists over a 4,888-provision, as-of-dated corpus spanning the DPDP Act 2023 and Rules 2025, the IT Act, company law, GST/TDS, labour/ESI and IP. (2) We show that LLM-only generation fabricates statutory citations (59 non-existent provisions; 16.8% unsupported claims). (3) We describe a LangGraph multi-agent pipeline with hybrid retrieval, grader-driven context selection, claim-level verification and scope abstention that reaches 0.776 provision recall and near-zero unsupported claims. (4) We validate these faithfulness measurements with a human audit and a cross-family judge.

The last clause is a promise the next three days must keep.

## Reviewers will attack the judge, the leakage and the baselines

Three objections are potentially fatal. Each has a fix that a student team can deliver on free-tier resources. The first is **the unvalidated, same-family LLM judge.** Strong LLM judges agree with humans more than 80% of the time on chat preference, but they show position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases ([arXiv 2306.05685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)). They also favour their own generations ([arXiv 2404.13076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)) and models from the same family ([arXiv 2508.06709](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06709)). PARCEL shows legal judges in particular over-accept unsupported claims ([arXiv 2610.10971](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10971)). Gemini judging Gemini is therefore the first thing an AI & Law reviewer will flag. Expect modest agreement numbers. A Colombian legal benchmark reported a judge-to-expert quadratic kappa of 0.635 ([arXiv 2610.03639](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.03639)), and LEXam found expert-to-expert agreement of only about 0.49 ([arXiv 2505.12864](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.12864)). With about 99% of pipeline claims labelled "supported", Cohen's kappa will be distorted by the kappa paradox, so report Gwet's AC1 alongside it ([arXiv 2509.12382](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.12382v1)). No published study was found that reports kappa for LLM-judged claim faithfulness in legal RAG, so a careful human audit is a small contribution in its own right.

The second is **test-set leakage.** The context selector was tuned on queries that overlap the evaluation set. Make the 26 never-tuned queries (0.942 vs 0.872) the honest headline and label the full-100 numbers as development. Better still, freeze 40–60 fresh queries written by someone who never saw the system and run the final configuration on them once. The third is **missing statistics and baselines.** Dror et al. found many ACL papers omit significance tests and recommend bootstrap or permutation tests for metrics like these ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/)). Reviewers will also expect at least one recognised method, Self-RAG- or CRAG-style, as a baseline ([arXiv 2401.15884](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15884)).

The secondary objections are cheaper to address. The 26% "partially supported" rate will read as overclaiming. ALCE itself notes that NLI scorers cannot handle partial support ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.398)), so report a strict metric and a lenient one, and use the audit to separate acceptable inference from real overreach. The n=10 out-of-scope set should grow to 30 or more, with 20 or more near-miss in-scope queries to measure false refusals. Without any legal expert, the paper should claim "provision-grounded" rather than "legally correct".

## Conclusion

The work's weak point is not the system, which already beats its closest Indian competitor on scale and features. The weak point is that its central faithfulness claim rests on an unaudited judge from the same model family. That inverts the usual priority: the highest-value work for the next 72 hours is measurement (human labels, a second judge, a frozen held-out set, confidence intervals), not engineering. A team that does this well can also present the audit itself as evidence. A pipeline that drives fabricated provisions to zero, *as confirmed by humans*, is a stronger and rarer result than another point of recall.

Two caveats temper the plan. Several venue metrics and 2026 preprint findings were seen only through snippets or aggregators, so verify them before they appear in a cover letter or a related-work section. And "first for Indian compliance workflows" rests on absence of evidence from searching, so keep the "to our knowledge" hedge. If AI & Law desk-rejects quickly, which its 6-day median makes likely to happen fast either way, the same validated manuscript transfers directly to IEEE Access or Discover AI. A substantially different demo version can then go to ICAIL 2027 in late January.
